Guiding principles

Three threads, woven together rather than run side by side.

When I first picked my three principles, I liked the ideas, but I was still trying to figure out what they had to do with my actual life. Over time they've started to feel more real — I can see them showing up in the way I work, the way I learn, and even in the things I worry about. I don't have everything figured out, but these principles give me something to come back to when I'm not sure what to do next.

1. Diligent work as an act of worship

One thing I've learned from freelance work is that people usually don't see all the work behind a project — they see whether it works, whether I meet my word, and whether they can trust me. That's made President Uchtdorf's teaching about work more personal: he describes work as an antidote for anxiety and a doorway to possibility. I don't think writing code automatically makes something spiritual. For me, the spiritual part is how I choose to do the work — if I say I'll do something, I want to do it; if I make a mistake, I want to admit it and fix it.

2. Continuous learning as a disciple's duty

One thing I like about IT is also one of the hardest parts of it: there's always more to learn. I can understand something today and discover tomorrow a completely different way to do it — sometimes exciting, sometimes I honestly feel behind. I'm learning that feeling behind doesn't mean I'm failing; it can just mean I found the next thing I need to learn. President Uchtdorf connects work and learning, and I like the idea that learning is part of how I take care of the abilities I've been given.

3. Trust in God through uncertainty

This is probably the principle closest to my life right now. I enjoy technology, especially AI, but I also know how quickly everything can change, and I don't know exactly what the IT industry will look like in five or ten years. What I'm learning is that I don't need to know every part of the future before I can move forward. I can keep learning, make good decisions, ask for help, and trust the Lord while I do my part.

Professional skills & spiritual beliefs

If I had to name where my technical work and my beliefs line up most directly right now, it would be AI. I hear a lot of people talk badly about AI, and some of that is fair — there's real harm it can cause. But I don't think the answer is to avoid it or only talk about the danger. I've come to three points I keep coming back to:

  • AI has real potential to hurt people — jobs, trust, privacy, truth. I don't think it's honest to pretend otherwise, and I've written about this earlier in the semester with the AI robocall case.
  • AI also has real potential to do good — I've seen this directly, lowering the cost of getting a small business online for people who couldn't have afforded a fully custom build otherwise.
  • We need to actively teach how to use it well, not just warn people away from it or let it be used carelessly. That's the part I think is missing most from the conversations I hear.

Those three points aren't just a professional opinion for me. They come from the same place as my guiding principles: work as worship means thinking about who a tool actually serves; trust in God through uncertainty means I don't have to have this all figured out to keep using AI carefully and honestly while I learn.

Ethical dilemma analysis

The situation

The ethical situation I chose involves an AI-generated voice used in a robocall in New Hampshire shortly before the 2024 presidential primary. The recording was made to sound like President Joe Biden and told voters not to vote in the primary. I chose this case because it's a clear example of technology being used in a way that can damage trust and create real-world consequences.

What happened and who was involved

Political consultant Steve Kramer was connected to creating and distributing the recording, and Lingo Telecom carried the calls. The Federal Communications Commission investigated the incident, and New Hampshire authorities also took legal action (Bond, 2024; Federal Communications Commission, 2024).

What I learned from the case

What stands out to me is how easy it had become to make false information sound believable. The technology itself wasn't the only issue — the bigger problem was the decision to use it to deceive people in a real situation where trust mattered. That's relevant to my own field because I work with AI-assisted tools that can produce information, summaries, or recommendations that people may trust. The response from regulators also showed that technology doesn't remove responsibility: the FCC proposed large fines and clarified that AI-generated voices used in robocalls can violate existing law, while New Hampshire authorities brought criminal charges.

This case also made me think about something more personal: AI can change the kind of work people do. A tool can make a business more efficient, but that doesn't mean the effect on workers is automatically positive. I don't have a perfect answer to that problem, but I can start by asking better questions. Who benefits? Who might be affected? Am I helping a person make a better decision, or am I trying to remove the person from the process? Those are questions I want to keep asking as I continue learning.

That made me think about my own life, because I've sometimes treated school, work, and spiritual growth as separate responsibilities. I realized I don't really want to live that way.

Elder Bednar's image of a rope made of intertwined strands helped me understand this better. I thought about the different parts of my own life: my education, my freelance work, my service, my temple experiences, and even my questions about the future of technology. I used to think of these as separate things happening at the same time. Now I'm starting to see how one can strengthen another — teaching has helped me become more patient, and patience helps when I'm stuck on a difficult technical problem; working with clients has made me better at listening, which matters when serving other people too.

The biggest thing I learned is that I don't want to manage two separate lives. I want to build one life where my professional skills, my faith, and my service support each other — not because every technical project needs a spiritual message attached, but because the principles I believe in should still affect me when I'm working, learning, making decisions, and dealing with people.
