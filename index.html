Guiding principles

Three threads, woven together rather than run side by side.

When I first picked my three principles, I liked the ideas, but I was still trying to figure out what they had to do with my actual life. Over time they've started to feel more real — I can see them showing up in the way I work, the way I learn, and even in the things I worry about. I don't have everything figured out, but these principles give me something to come back to when I'm not sure what to do next.

1. Diligent work as an act of worship

One thing I've learned from freelance work is that people usually don't see all the work behind a project — they see whether it works, whether I meet my word, and whether they can trust me. That's made President Uchtdorf's teaching about work more personal: he describes work as an antidote for anxiety and a doorway to possibility. I don't think writing code automatically makes something spiritual. For me, the spiritual part is how I choose to do the work — if I say I'll do something, I want to do it; if I make a mistake, I want to admit it and fix it.

2. Continuous learning as a disciple's duty

One thing I like about IT is also one of the hardest parts of it: there's always more to learn. I can understand something today and discover tomorrow a completely different way to do it — sometimes exciting, sometimes I honestly feel behind. I'm learning that feeling behind doesn't mean I'm failing; it can just mean I found the next thing I need to learn. President Uchtdorf connects work and learning, and I like the idea that learning is part of how I take care of the abilities I've been given.

3. Trust in God through uncertainty

This is probably the principle closest to my life right now. I enjoy technology, especially AI, but I also know how quickly everything can change, and I don't know exactly what the IT industry will look like in five or ten years. What I'm learning is that I don't need to know every part of the future before I can move forward. I can keep learning, make good decisions, ask for help, and trust the Lord while I do my part.

Professional skills & spiritual beliefs

If I had to name where my technical work and my beliefs line up most directly right now, it would be AI. I hear a lot of people talk badly about AI, and some of that is fair — there's real harm it can cause. But I don't think the answer is to avoid it or only talk about the danger. I've come to three points I keep coming back to:

  • AI has real potential to hurt people — jobs, trust, privacy, truth. I don't think it's honest to pretend otherwise, and I've written about this earlier in the semester with the AI robocall case.
  • AI also has real potential to do good — I've seen this directly, lowering the cost of getting a small business online for people who couldn't have afforded a fully custom build otherwise.
  • We need to actively teach how to use it well, not just warn people away from it or let it be used carelessly. That's the part I think is missing most from the conversations I hear.

Those three points aren't just a professional opinion for me. They come from the same place as my guiding principles: work as worship means thinking about who a tool actually serves; trust in God through uncertainty means I don't have to have this all figured out to keep using AI carefully and honestly while I learn.

Project refinement & ethical integration

For this refinement, I went back to the Inventory Organizer / AI-Enhanced Data Administration project above and rebuilt it with the technical and ethical safeguards it was missing the first time. The original version worked, but it applied AI-generated reorder suggestions with no review step, had no login system or audit trail, and exported client data the same way regardless of who was asking.

Challenges identified

  • Security — no authentication existed at all, and early logic wasn't consistently protected against SQL injection.
  • Accountability — nothing recorded who changed what, or when.
  • Scalability — the dashboard loaded every item at once, which wouldn't hold up for a larger catalog.
  • Accessibility — plain HTML with no labelled fields or status announcements.

Ethical considerations integrated

  • Over-automation and job displacement — an AI feature that changes inventory on its own reduces the role of the person who used to do that job by hand, even when it's accurate.
  • Data privacy — the original export included client-identifying data every time, regardless of who requested it or why.
  • Explainability and trust — a bare AI number with no reasoning asks a client to trust output they can't evaluate.
  • Accountability — with no login system, no action could be traced to a responsible person.

Refinements made

Each of these is implemented in the actual code, not just described:

  • Human-in-the-loop approval — AI reorder suggestions sit in a review queue and never change inventory until an admin explicitly approves them.
  • Role-based access control — staff can view and run AI analysis; only admins can approve suggestions or export identifying data.
  • Full audit logging — every login, AI run, and approval decision is timestamped and attributed.
  • Explainable, rule-based suggestions — each one comes with a plain-language rationale and a confidence level, not just a number.
  • Privacy-conscious export — anonymized by default; full detail requires an admin to type a logged justification first.
  • Security hardening — parameterized queries throughout and hashed passwords.
  • Accessibility improvements — labelled form fields and an ARIA live region for status messages.
  • Pagination — the dashboard now loads items in pages instead of all at once.

Code: human-in-the-loop approval

Nothing changes inventory automatically — a suggestion only becomes real once a named admin approves it, and that decision is logged.

def review_suggestion(suggestion_id, decision):

Impact and decision-making

The most important decision here was resisting the temptation to make the AI feature more automated instead of more accountable. It would have been simpler to let confident suggestions apply themselves — and that version would have demoed better. I chose human-in-the-loop instead because it's the more honest response to the ethical concern I kept coming back to all semester: that AI efficiency in my own field can come at the cost of someone's role unless a person deliberately stays in the loop. The system is slower now — nothing happens without a click of approval — but it's more trustworthy: a client can see exactly who approved a change, why the AI suggested it, and what data left the system.

Peer review

I reviewed Jesse Moroni Milton's project, Heritage Vault 2.0 — a system architecture proposal for secure genealogical record storage.

Technical assessment

The refinements are well-scoped: a tiered visibility system (public / family-only / private), a consent flag for records of living individuals, continuous security monitoring, accessibility requirements, and a "vault successor" inheritance role. Tying the security refinement directly to a real, named case (the 2018 MyHeritage breach) rather than a generic "we added security" statement was a strength. My main technical note was that, as a proposal rather than an implementation, a few claims — like how tiered visibility is enforced at the data layer, and what specific data-model change solves the scalability problem — could use more concrete detail.

Ethical integration evaluation

This was the strongest part of the submission. The consent question — that a living relative didn't necessarily agree to have her information stored in a system she doesn't control — is specific and well-reasoned, not a generic privacy statement. I raised one open question in return: the consent flag requires acknowledgment before sharing a living relative's record, but it isn't clear who is empowered to grant that consent on the relative's behalf when they aren't the one using the system.

Constructive feedback given

  • Technical: name the specific data-model approach (e.g., graph-based structure) that solves the scalability challenge.
  • Ethical: name who can exercise consent on behalf of a living person who isn't the vault's user.
  • What I'm borrowing: tying each refinement to a specific, named real-world case rather than describing it in the abstract — Jesse did this more consistently than I did in my own first draft.

Ethical dilemma analysis

The situation

The ethical situation I chose involves an AI-generated voice used in a robocall in New Hampshire shortly before the 2024 presidential primary. The recording was made to sound like President Joe Biden and told voters not to vote in the primary. I chose this case because it's a clear example of technology being used in a way that can damage trust and create real-world consequences.

What happened and who was involved

Political consultant Steve Kramer was connected to creating and distributing the recording, and Lingo Telecom carried the calls. The Federal Communications Commission investigated the incident, and New Hampshire authorities also took legal action (Bond, 2024; Federal Communications Commission, 2024).

What I learned from the case

What stands out to me is how easy it had become to make false information sound believable. The technology itself wasn't the only issue — the bigger problem was the decision to use it to deceive people in a real situation where trust mattered. That's relevant to my own field because I work with AI-assisted tools that can produce information, summaries, or recommendations that people may trust. The response from regulators also showed that technology doesn't remove responsibility: the FCC proposed large fines and clarified that AI-generated voices used in robocalls can violate existing law, while New Hampshire authorities brought criminal charges.

This case also made me think about something more personal: AI can change the kind of work people do. A tool can make a business more efficient, but that doesn't mean the effect on workers is automatically positive. I don't have a perfect answer to that problem, but I can start by asking better questions. Who benefits? Who might be affected? Am I helping a person make a better decision, or am I trying to remove the person from the process? Those are questions I want to keep asking as I continue learning.

A better alternative: leading with responsibility

The principle I think was most missing from this situation is responsibility — a clear answer to who is accountable when an AI tool causes harm. I've seen this question up close in my own work: I've been part of a business that used AI heavily to code faster, and the question we kept coming back to was, if the AI produces something wrong or harmful, who takes the blame — the company using it, or the tool itself? I don't think that question has a comfortable answer, but I do think the responsibility has to sit with the person or company deploying the tool, not the tool.

If Kramer genuinely wanted to warn the public about the dangers of AI-generated voices, a responsible version of that same goal existed: he could have published a clearly labeled example demonstrating the technology, explained the risk directly to election officials and the public, or worked with the campaign and regulators to raise the concern before the primary — not during it, and not disguised as the real thing. That path keeps the warning honest instead of becoming the harm it claims to warn about.

I also don't think the right response to a case like this is to only talk about the danger of AI. The same fear showed up when the internet first became widespread, and again with social media — people worried Facebook would become an addiction, and in some ways it has been. But as Elder David A. Bednar taught in Things as They Really Are 2.0, technology only enslaves us if we let it; we remain agents, capable of choosing how we engage with it. I think the same is true of AI. Miscommunication is something AI does fairly often — it can sound confident while being wrong — but that same flexibility is also what makes it useful for good when someone stays responsible for checking and correcting it.

Applying this to the robocall case: the better outcome for voters is obvious (no deception), but it's also a better outcome for the candidate Kramer worked for (no association with an election crime) and for public trust in AI generally, since the story becomes "here's a risk we identified and handled responsibly" instead of "here's how easily AI can be weaponized against you." Responsibility, not avoidance, is what actually protects people.

My view, and how I'll handle this in my own work

Working through this case has clarified something I already suspected but hadn't said out loud: AI doesn't remove responsibility from a situation, it just makes it easier to forget who's holding it. That's true of a political consultant faking a candidate's voice, and it's true in much smaller, more ordinary moments in my own freelance work.

A client almost cut a project short partway through because he realized he could probably use AI to build something similar himself. He wasn't wrong — he could get an AI tool to generate a working site. What he didn't have was the years of judgment that tell me when a generated solution is quietly wrong, insecure, or going to cause him problems in six months. I didn't try to talk him out of using AI, because I don't think that would have been honest. Instead, I helped him see the actual difference: a professional using AI and someone without that background using the same tool will not end up with the same result, even if both results look similar at first. He decided to stay on the project once he understood that the value was never just "can someone type this into an AI," it was the judgment behind deciding what to type, when to trust the output, and when to override it.

Going forward, that's the approach I want to keep: not hiding from the fact that I use AI in my own work, and not pretending it makes expertise unnecessary either. When a similar situation comes up again, I want to be upfront about where AI is helping me move faster, and just as clear about where my own judgment is the part actually being paid for. That feels like the same principle as the robocall case, just at a much smaller scale — responsibility has to stay attached to a person, not get outsourced to the tool.

Christlike attributes & personal warning signs

The attribute I want to focus on right now is diligence. For me, diligence and trusting God aren't two separate things — trusting God is actually one of the clearest ways I show diligence. If I really trusted the outcome was up to me alone, I'd have a reason to panic every time something got hard. Instead, continuing to show up, keep working, and keep learning even when I can't see how it resolves is itself an act of trust. That's the same thing I noticed in the temple experience I reflected on a few weeks ago: I didn't stop working, I just stopped trying to carry the outcome by myself.

The warning sign I've had to get honest about is stress — specifically, not always noticing when my stress level has crossed from "pushing me to work harder" into "actually hurting my ability to work at all." I tend to notice it late, usually once my patience with people is already thinner than it should be, or once the quality of my work has already started slipping. Luke 21:19 says, "In your patience possess ye your souls," and this week that verse has felt less like general advice and more like a direct description of what I'm missing — patience with myself, specifically, as a warning sign starts to build, instead of only noticing it once it's already a problem.

The strategy I'm trying to build isn't complicated: catch it earlier. When I notice myself rushing, getting short with people, or dreading a task I'd normally enjoy, that's my signal to stop, take a breath, and often say a short prayer before doing anything else — the same small pause that helped me during that temple shift. It doesn't fix the workload by itself, but it resets whether I'm meeting the workload with diligence or with panic, and I've learned those produce very different results.

Usability testing & design enhancements

Before making changes, I shared the live site with a couple of friends and asked them to look through it on both a computer and a phone, without giving them much direction beyond "tell me what you notice."

What I heard

The response was encouraging on the content side — they liked the continuity from section to section, and one friend specifically said the page felt very insightful in how it handled each topic and the material behind it, rather than feeling like a list of disconnected assignments. That told me the narrative structure I'd been building all semester was actually landing the way I hoped.

The problem I found

Reviewing the site myself with that feedback in mind, I found a real usability gap: on a phone, the navigation menu disappeared completely below a certain screen width, with no alternative way to jump between sections. Anyone visiting on mobile — which is likely most first-time visitors clicking a link from a phone — could only scroll blindly with no way to see the page's structure or jump to a specific section like Projects or Résumé.

The enhancement I made

I added a mobile menu button (three lines, the standard pattern people already recognize) that opens a simple dropdown list of the same section links used on desktop. It's a small, targeted fix rather than a redesign — I didn't want to disrupt the continuity my friends specifically said was working. The impact is that mobile visitors, probably the majority of the people who will actually open this link, can now navigate the site with the same structure desktop visitors already had, instead of losing the site's organization entirely.

Faith as a seed: a visual mental map

For this week's reflective activity, I kept coming back to Alma's discourse on faith as a seed (Alma 32). It's a passage I've read many times, but mapping it directly onto my own life — instead of just the general idea of "gaining a testimony" — made it feel much more specific to where I actually am right now, professionally and spiritually.

Plant the seed

Alma 32:28 — trying "even a particle of faith" as an experiment.

This was choosing to start: enrolling at Ensign College, taking on a freelance client with no guarantee it would work out, sitting down with a stressed-out client instead of walking away. Every one of those started as an experiment I hadn't proven yet.

Not cast out by unbelief

Alma 32:28 — if you do not cast it out by your unbelief.

This is the stress-management warning sign above, in practice. The seed doesn't get a chance to grow if I quit the moment doubt or panic shows up. Staying at my post during that temple shift, instead of walking away from the problem, was choosing not to cast it out.

Swells, sprouts, enlarges the soul

Alma 32:28 — "it beginneth to enlarge my soul... to enlighten my understanding."

The early evidence: the temple workers I needed arriving that same shift, a client staying on the project once he understood the difference professional judgment makes, a seminary student opening up after I started calling to check in personally. Small, but real signs it was working.

Nourished with diligence and patience

Alma 32:41 — nourish the tree "with great care, that it may get root."

This is where diligence and Luke 21:19's patience actually do their work — not in the exciting moment of starting something, but in the repeated, unglamorous effort afterward: continuing to study, continuing client relationships past the first hard conversation, continuing to teach seminary students long after the first breakthrough.

A tree bearing fruit "precious above all"

Alma 32:42 — you shall "feast upon this fruit... which is sweet above all that is sweet."

This is where I want it all to end up: not just a working portfolio or a stable career, but something that actually blesses other people — small businesses that can now afford to be online, family history work that connects real relatives, students who see a future for themselves. The fruit was never supposed to be for me alone.
